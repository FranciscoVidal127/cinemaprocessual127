*I was very touched by how your film dealt with the idea of conserving and preserving the natural world. What drew you to this theme and how has it developed over your career as an artist?*

**Marianna Milhorat:** Throughout my work I have been very interested in the shifting relationships to landscape, and with this film I've particularly turned to the conservation landscape. I started with this premise of what it means to observe modern conservation efforts and some of the absurd images that result from those efforts. But then I spent time in the field with participants and became really moved by the experience of what they were actually doing — what it is like to spend an hour in the field listening to frogs with a group of retired volunteers. And so I started thinking more about the nature of hope and empathy. And how these elements can be possibilities for having a different kind of future — including different relationships with non-human beings.

*How did you come across this specific group of people? I was very moved by how these participants presented themselves, specially one of the main protagonists, Marnie Baker, (who sadly passed away recently) who writes and recites haiku poetry during the course of the film. How did you meet her? And how long did it take for this process to transform into the film itself?*

**Marianna Milhorat:** Well, at the time I was beginning the film I was living in Chicago in the U.S. In the mid-2000s, when white nose syndrome, a disease affecting bats, began appearing in the US, it started on the east coast and gradually spread west. And I connected with some of these citizen science programs and they connected me with different individual groups. So the frog-monitoring initiative happened through the Peggy Notebaert Nature Museum, in Chicago. And I particularly loved the group of women who monitored frogs in the film. They had a unique dynamic among themselves and were very welcoming, allowing me to spend time with them.

*One thing that I felt while watching was the experience of a very immersive way of filmmaking... How did the visual language of the film develop over its making?*

**Marianna Milhorat:** Yeah, so I was really interested in creating these movements from the more observational style to the more lyrical one. The goal was for audiences to shift from observing participants to becoming observers themselves. During the extended scene of women listening to frogs for five minutes, viewers can start by looking at them, observing them and thinking about who they are, and then themselves become a person listening to the frogs alongside them. I explicitly did not want to make a purely informative film about species decline data. My goal was to create these moments of embodied experience for the audience — this idea of creating moments of embodied experience for the audience is to be a gateway to new forms of empathy and new ways of relating to the spaces around us.

*Seeing this film in Brazil, with a very receptive Brazilian audience, was very moving... are there any other places in the world, or in your own country, that you would be interested to go and create new films?*

**Marianna Milhorat:** Yeah, absolutely, filming in Amazon would be incredible! Currently I am thinking of creating a new work centered in Quebec — centered on the aquatic dead zones of that region, the midpoint where the St. Lawrence River meets the ocean. And at the moment, I am at the beginning of post-production of a work that was filmed in Finland — a behavioral test event for dogs, in which the dogs encounter these large remote-controlled taxidermied copies of bears and wolves.

*How has been the experience of sharing this film in festivals, and how do you see the reception and importance of the film with the current political and environmental challenges that our world is facing?*

**Marianna Milhorat:** Well, the film is actually at the very beginning of its festival run. The Ecrã Film Festival was the film's premiere, with European and U.S. premieres scheduled for August. I am fascinated by the community-specific conversations the film generates, and I'm eager to follow audience reception as the film circulates globally.
